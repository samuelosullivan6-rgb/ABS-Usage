Part one · for the dugout

When to spend a token

A challenge token is not worth a fixed amount. It is worth what it can still buy over the rest of the game. At first pitch the first token is worth about 0.15 runs and the second about 0.09; by the 9th the last token is worth about 0.04. Savant's breakeven rule prices every token at 0.20 runs. For a league-average eye that is more than a token is worth at any point in the game, so the rule is too stingy throughout: challenge more readily than it says, early as well as late, and more readily still as the game goes on.

  1. Early, with both tokens, a 33% chance the call flips is enough on a typical pitch. With the last token early, about 44%. Savant's price asks for 51%. To an average eye that is a pitch that looks about 1.6 inches off the plate with two tokens and 3.1 with one.
  2. Strike three and ball four are the big stakes. On a 3-2 count with runners on a flip is worth half a run, and early with both tokens a 16% chance is enough.
  3. In the 9th with your last token, about 19% is enough. An unused token at the final out is worth nothing, and a team at zero gets one back if the game goes on.
  4. Extra innings give a team at zero one token back each inning. A token in an extra inning is worth about 0.04 runs, the same as in the 9th, so the 9th-inning rule is the rule for the rest of the game.
  5. These numbers are for league-average eyes. Sharper eyes make a token worth more: with perceptual noise of 1.5 inches instead of 2.6, the first token at first pitch is worth 0.23 runs, above Savant's price, and early in the game the bar moves back toward Savant's.

Revised 2026-10-23. An earlier version of this page valued the first token at 0.49 runs and said to be stingy early. That came from a belief model in which a challenger who felt 90% sure was right about half the time. With beliefs that know how often the umpire is right, the first token is worth 0.15 and the early advice reverses.

Hitter challenge cards

Every called strike a hitter takes with a token in hand is a test of his eyes: the true location is known, and he either challenges or he doesn't. The eyes score is how sharply his challenge rate rises as the pitch moves from inside the zone to outside it, against the league. The criterion is how freely he spends tokens. The margins are how far off the plate a pitch should look to him before he challenges on a typical pitch, early with both tokens, early with the last token, mid-game, and in the 9th.

Eyes case or approach case

A hitter who chases has one of two problems. Either he does not see the pitch well enough to know it is a ball, or he sees it fine and swings anyway. The fix differs: pitch-recognition work for the first, a count plan for the second. Chase rate alone cannot separate them because every swing mixes perception with intent. The challenge decision on a take has no swing in it.

The sort in the cards uses both: a hitter in the top 40% of chase rate with a below-median eyes score is an Eyes case; the same chaser with an above-median eyes score is an Approach case. Hitters in the bottom 40% of chase rate are Disciplined.

What the evidence supports. Sharper eyes on takes go with chasing less (correlation -0.21 across the 223 rated regulars, stronger among frequent challengers) and with cutting the chase rate from the prior season. They do not go with cheaper chases, better two-strike contact or fewer strikeouts once chase rate is held fixed. So the sort tells you which lever moves the rate; it does not say who pays most per chase.

What the belief model does

The challenger's belief is the probability the call flips given the edge they perceive. The calibrated belief is fit on the opportunities themselves, so it knows the umpire is usually right: in every fifth of its range it is within 0.3 points of the true flip rate. The flat-prior belief, P = Φ(perceived edge / noise), was used on this page before 2026-10-23. It ignores the umpire's base rate, and a pitch it calls 80 to 100% likely flips about half the time.

Sensitivity to the noise level

The noise level, 2.6 inches, comes from the league eyes slope and is an upper bound (the slope also absorbs criterion variation). Under the flat prior the token's value barely moves with it; under calibrated beliefs it does, and below about 1.8 inches the first token at first pitch is worth more than Savant's 0.20.

Methods and caveats

Data
MLB StatsAPI live feeds carry every ABS challenge at the pitch level with the initiating player and outcome (review type MJ). Per-team counts reconcile with the in-game token ledger in all but one game. Statcast CSVs supply run values and bat tracking, joined on every pitch.
Zone
The feed's strike-zone bounds are the fixed ABS zone (53.5% and 27% of measured height). Ball location is solved at the plate midpoint from the nine-parameter trajectory; the signed edge distance uses a 2.9-inch ball and reproduces Savant's values exactly. It agrees with the ABS ruling on 99.99% of challenged pitches.
Decision set
Umpire-called strikes the hitter took with a token in hand, within 4 inches of the edge: 65,589 pitches, 4,609 challenged.
Judgment model
Logit of challenge on edge distance with per-hitter random intercept and slope, fit by empirical Bayes; count, token and leverage shifters as fixed effects. The slope is unidentified for hitters who rarely challenge.
Token value
Backward induction over eight decision slots per half-inning for nine innings, with opportunities arriving at the observed rate (1.34 per team-half-inning: taken pitches within 3 inches of the edge, both sides) and stakes from Statcast run expectancy. Extra innings enter as a continuation value at the last out of the 9th: 209 of 2,429 games went to the 10th, each extra inning is followed by another with probability 0.27, and a team at zero gets one token back at the start of every extra inning, as the ledgers show. The simulated rules play the same token rule.
Belief
A challenger perceives the true edge plus Gaussian noise and holds the calibrated posterior P(flip | perceived edge), a logit of the true flip on the perceived edge fit with the same noise draw. The per-hitter margins use the same fit at each hitter's own noise level.
Caveats
One season of ABS. Run units, not win probability, so late close games are undervalued. The simulated rules use league-average Gaussian perception; the observed row is real players, who know more than that model. Under calibrated beliefs the token's value depends on the noise level, which is an upper bound. The eyes score needs about 20 challenges to be reliable per hitter. Year-over-year judgment and outcome share a season.
