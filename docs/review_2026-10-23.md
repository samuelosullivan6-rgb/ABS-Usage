# Review of the ABS investigation

Date: 2026-10-23. Scope: the `ABS INVESTIGATION` project (README, docs, src, scripts, tests, the club brief and the processed tables) and the ABS-related parts of the Chase Leaderboard (step 04's called-strike model, the 2026 zone, the README's 2026 limitation). Every number below was recomputed from the projects' own data; the scripts are `check_a_judgment.py`, `check_b_yoy_and_zone.py`, `check_c_policy.py`, `check_c2_sigma.py` and `check_d2_direction_bias.py` in this session's scratchpad.

## Verdict in five lines

1. The data layer is excellent and verified: 10,557 challenges recovered (Savant's total), the token ledger matched in 2,428 of 2,429 games, the geometry agrees with the ABS ruling on 99.99% of challenged pitches, the Savant attribution reproduces (99.4% of batters exact), the 20 tests pass.
2. The token-value table and the "when to spend a token" guidance rest on a belief model that is badly miscalibrated. With coherent beliefs the first token is worth 0.15 runs at first pitch, not 0.49, and the dugout advice reverses.
3. The "eyes" score is not a measurement of perception. It is a logistic slope fit on the lower tail of a curve whose midpoint lies outside the data window for 71% of hitters, pooled across directions whose challenge rates differ by two to one at the same true distance, with reliability 0.27 at the threshold where the brief assigns Eyes and Approach labels.
4. The one positive result survives the zone-change confound I tested, slightly attenuated. It is also one of 27 tests, and it is not significant at the thresholds where the score is reliable.
5. The brief prints "+nan" in its headline sentence, and both the brief and the README describe an extra-innings rule in the token model that the code never runs.

For the Chase Leaderboard: the README's "2026 is harder to compare ... can't say why" is now answerable with numbers, and the direction-blind called-strike model's error more than doubled in 2026.

## A. The policy: token value and the static-versus-dynamic comparison

**A1. The belief model is miscalibrated, and everything downstream inherits it.** The simulated challenger's subjective probability is p = Phi(perceived edge / sigma), the posterior under a flat prior over the true edge. But the umpire is right on 80% of near-edge called pitches (wrong on 20.2% of the 115,389 opportunities), and a coherent challenger knows that. Calibration of the project's p against the true flip rate:

| simulated p | n | true flip rate |
|---|---:|---:|
| 0.8 to 1.0 | 14,765 | 49% |
| 0.6 to 0.8 | 16,379 | 32% |
| 0.4 to 0.6 | 19,233 | 22% |
| 0.2 to 0.4 | 23,681 | 15% |

A challenger who says "90%" and is right 49% of the time is not a model of anyone. The internal inconsistency shows in the project's own numbers: the DP values two tokens at the start of a game at 0.720 runs (117 runs per team-season), and the same policy, played forward, realizes 35.2.

Replacing p with a calibrated posterior (a logit of the true flip on the perceived edge, same noise draw, same sigma) changes the conclusions, not just the sizes:

| | first token, first pitch | static rule: runs / challenges per team-game / overturned | dynamic rule | dynamic minus static | dynamic minus observed |
|---|---:|---|---|---:|---:|
| project (flat prior) | 0.487 | 32.6 / 3.10 / 39.5% | 35.2 / 3.29 / 40.0% | +2.65 ± 0.34 | +2.34 ± 0.68 |
| calibrated beliefs | 0.149 | 19.1 / 0.75 / 53.9% | 38.2 / 2.89 / 40.2% | +19.1 ± 0.5 | +5.3 ± 0.7 |

(Standard errors are paired by game over 2,429 games; two other seeds give +2.25 and +2.63 for the project's setup.) Under calibrated beliefs the token is worth less than Savant's 0.20 at every moment of the game, so the correct advice relative to Savant's rule is "more aggressive always," not the brief's "be stingy early, aggressive late." The dugout table's certainties and inches ("82% sure early with two tokens, about 2.4 inches off") are wrong in level, not just noisy.

**A2. "With the same eyes" is false for the observed row.** Static and dynamic share the simulated eyes; "observed" is real players, who overturned 54% of 2.04 challenges a game. The league-average-noise model cannot produce that: under calibrated beliefs only 2,326 of 115,389 opportunities carry p > 0.6, yet players found 9,901 challenges and won 5,352. Real players hold information the sigma model lacks, so the 2.3-run gap to observed is not a property of the rule.

**A3. The one parameter that matters was never varied.** The README's "perceptual noise of about 2.6 inches" is 1.7 divided by the league logit slope, which decision 9 admits is an upper bound (the slope is attenuated by criterion variation, by direction mixing, see B2, and by uncertainty about where the invisible box is). The sensitivity the repo could have run but did not: under the flat prior, the first token's value sits between 0.45 and 0.53 runs for sigma from 1.0 to 4.5 inches and dynamic minus static between +1.6 and +2.8, while the runs level swings from 66.5 to 23.6. The conclusion is insensitive to sigma and entirely sensitive to the belief model.

**A4. The extra-innings top-up in the DP is dead code.** `token_value_dp` has T = 18 × 8 = 144 slots, so the branch `t // slots >= 18` never fires and V(0, t) = 0 everywhere. The brief's Methods ("a per-inning top-up in extra innings as observed in the ledgers") and the README's Bellman note describe a model that was not run. The simulation does implement the top-up, so the DP's thresholds and the simulation's token accounting disagree with each other. 209 of 2,429 games went to extras.

**A5. Where the rules differ.** Challenges per team-game by inning band:

| innings | static | dynamic | observed |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 to 3 | 1.79 | 1.59 | 0.58 |
| 4 to 6 | 0.89 | 1.03 | 0.67 |
| 7 and 8 | 0.30 | 0.43 | 0.49 |
| 9 and later | 0.12 | 0.23 | 0.30 |

Both simulated rules challenge three times as often as real teams in the first three innings. Real teams already behave more like the dynamic rule than the static one, which undercuts the brief's framing that teams hoard too much late; the data say they are far more conservative early than either rule, and that conservatism is largely rational given A1.

**A6. Per-hitter margins are false precision.** `sigma_i = 1.7 / max(slope, 0.05)` uses a slope whose reliability is 0.27 to 0.31 at the 8-challenge threshold the brief uses; 42% of hitters have a posterior slope SD above 0.15 against a between-hitter SD of 0.176, so their personal sigma is mostly the prior. The brief prints each hitter's margin to a tenth of an inch.

**A7. Smaller things.** Savant's 0.2-run price is taken from memory, not verified in the repo. Only one seed is run and no standard error is reported anywhere in the policy section (they are 0.3 to 0.7 runs, see A1). The "never" rule is computed and never used.

## B. The judgment model: what the "eyes" slope measures

**B1. The curve is fit on its tail.** League intercept −2.93, slope 0.65 per inch: the 50% point is at +4.5 inches, outside the ±4 inch window the model is fit on. The median hitter's 50% point is 4.6 inches and 71% of hitters have theirs beyond 4; the league challenge rate at 3.5 to 4 inches outside is 39%. On the tail of a logistic, slope and intercept are nearly collinear: the data constrain a + b × (mean edge of the challenged pitches), not b on its own. That is why the criterion's split-half reliability is 0.78 and the slope's 0.27, and why d-prime's is 0.11. Signal detection needs both tails; this experiment observes one.

**B2. Direction is not in the model, and it matters more than anything that is.** At the same true distance, hitters challenge a low called strike about twice as often as a high one:

| true edge, inches outside | above | below | side | corner |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 0 to 1 | 9.5% | 21.3% | 13.5% | 22.1% |
| 1 to 2 | 17.4% | 29.2% | 20.6% | 34.5% |
| 2 to 4 | 28.1% | 41.3% | 26.7% | 33.3% |

Overturn rates differ the same way (above 60%, below 47%, side 45%, corner 71%). A likelihood-ratio test of direction × edge against the pooled model gives chi-square 368 on 6 df; adding pitch group on top adds only 14 on 6 df, which is a point in the model's favor. The slope interactions are small (below −0.04, side −0.05, corner −0.27 per inch); the big differences are the intercepts. So a hitter's "criterion" is partly his stimulus mix, and through B1 so is his "eyes."

**B3. The score mixes perception with knowledge of where the box is.** The ABS box is invisible and sits inside the umpire's zone: in 2026 the called-strike rate on takes 0 to 1 inch above the box is 48.5%, 1 to 2 inches 27%, and the 50% point is half an inch above the box at the top. A hitter deciding whether to challenge a high strike is deciding where the box is, not only where the ball was. A hitter who still carries the traditional zone in his head under-challenges high pitches (above: 5.2% challenged, 21% true balls) through a calibration error that information would fix, not perceptual noise. Two correlations say the score is not pure eyes: judgment_z correlates −0.16 with a hitter's share of true-ball called strikes and −0.20 with his mean edge. Hitters the umpires miss against more look "shallower."

**B4. Players learned during the season.** The challenge rate on true balls rose from 16% in March to 21% by June and stayed there; the batter overturn rate rose from 46% in April to 52% in August. The split-half compares a learning period with a settled one, so the reliability figures are a little pessimistic and the "stable trait" claim for the criterion partly says "everyone moved together."

**B5. The reliability-by-volume table rests on 28 hitters.** The 0.79 at ten-plus challenges per half is Spearman-Brown of r ≈ 0.65 with a standard error near 0.11, so the full-season figure sits anywhere from about 0.6 to 0.93. Requiring ten challenges in both halves also selects on criterion stability, and judgment_z correlates +0.14 with challenge count. The brief states "accuracy becomes reliable at about 20 challenges" as a fact.

**B6. The brief labels hitters with the noisy score.** "Eyes case" and "Approach case" are assigned at 8+ challenges, where the score is roughly 70% noise; 66 hitters carry one of those labels. "Provisional" is marked under 15 challenges but the label stands. The headline sentence of that section reads "(correlation +nan across regulars ...)": `render()` correlates over hitters with 8+ challenges, some of whom have no judgment row (judgment_z is None after the left merge), so the published page says nan. Nothing checks the rendered output.

**B7. The decision window drops the most informative pitches.** 39 batter challenges on pitches more than 4 inches outside the box are excluded; those are the clearest misses and the strongest evidence of a sharp eye. 35 more are dropped because the token state is reconstructed without the extras top-up. Small numbers, but the window should be asymmetric.

**B8. The estimator.** Two-step empirical Bayes: fixed effects from a pooled logit with no random effects, then held; the prior mean replaced by the mean over hitters with 60+ decisions; a diagonal prior for intercept and slope although their posteriors are strongly correlated (B1). Standard and transparent, as decision 7 says, but the slope-intercept correlation it suppresses is the identification problem itself. A mixed logit with a full covariance would at least report it.

**B9. In the model's favor.** The criterion is individual, not a team policy: ICC by team 0.05 (F 1.38, p 0.10); judgment 0.02. Pitch type barely matters once direction is in (B2). The psychometric framing is the right idea; it needs the box, the direction and both tails.

## C. The eyes-versus-approach test

**C1. The one positive result survives the obvious confound.** The 2026 Statcast zone is the fixed ABS box, 2.85 inches shorter than the same hitters' 2025 stance zones (top −2.6 inches, bottom +0.3, SD across hitters 1.0 inch), so a hitter's 2025-to-2026 "change" includes a bookkeeping shift that varies by hitter and correlates −0.12 with the measured change. I applied each hitter's 2026 box to his 2025 pitches: the league's 2025 chase rate goes from 28.0% to 30.1%, so 2.1 of the 2.6-point rise the README reports is the box and about half a point is behavior. Rerunning the project's regression (n = 175, my rebuilt 2026 chase rate matches the project's to four decimals):

| control for the 2025 chase rate | coefficient per SD of judgment | p |
|---|---:|---:|
| project's spec (2025 stance zone) | −0.0100 | 0.012 |
| plus zone-shift controls | −0.0093 | 0.016 |
| 2025 rate measured against the 2026 box | −0.0087 | 0.026 |
| same-box plus zone-shift controls | −0.0087 | 0.027 |

The confound is real (the top shift predicts the 2026 rate at −0.0063 per inch, p = 0.026) but it is not the explanation. Credit where due. At 15+ challenges the same-box coefficient is −0.0045 (p = 0.40) and at 20+ it is −0.0074 (p = 0.28), so the result exists only where the score is unreliable.

**C2. Multiplicity and disattenuation.** Nine outcomes at three thresholds plus the baseline variant, and the README highlights the single p below 0.05. The "disattenuated" column divides every coefficient by 0.27, tripling the nulls as well, and reports them ("−0.48 SD") in a way that invites over-reading.

**C3. The outcome for chase cost is the wrong one.** "Runs per 100 chase swings" is Statcast's delta_run_exp, which is RE24-based and depends on runners and outs. The Chase Leaderboard already prices these same hitters' chases context-neutral. Using it would make the test stronger and tie the two projects together.

**C4. "Unbiased, not precise" is not how shrinkage works.** A posterior mean is pulled toward the prior in proportion to each hitter's sample, and the sample (challenges) depends on how often the hitter takes, which is related to chase rate. The measurement error is neither classical nor independent of the controls, so a null here is weaker evidence than the design doc claims.

## D. Code and report hygiene

- The zone test fetches game feeds from StatsAPI when its cache is cold; tests should not need the network.
- `rulebook_strike` in `chase.py` adds a ball radius vertically; the Chase Leaderboard's zone adds none. The two projects define "rulebook chase" differently and neither says so.
- `scripts/10_reliability_checks.py` hard-codes the season label.
- `fit_outcome` bootstraps the coefficient but reports HC1 p-values; the README should say which it quotes.
- The brief's "Extra innings give a team at zero one token back each inning" comes from the ledger analysis, not from the token model (A4).
- Every table in the brief names its source file. That is good practice and worth keeping.

## E. What this means for the Chase Leaderboard

**E1. The 2026 limitation can now be stated as fact.** Statcast's 2026 sz_top and sz_bot are the ABS box: the within-batter SD of sz_top is 0.000 in 2026 against 0.07 to 0.10 feet in 2021 to 2025, and the recorded zone is 2.86 inches shorter than in 2025 (top 3.435 to 3.215 feet, bottom 1.605 to 1.622). Umpires kept calling a taller zone. The called-strike rate on takes 0 to 2 inches above the recorded top is 75.7% in 2026 against 41 to 58% in the five seasons before, and the top edge's 50% point sits 1.8 inches above the recorded box against 0.6 to 1.2 before.

**E2. The consequences in the main project.**

- Chase rate 28.3% to 30.5%: about 2.1 points of that is the box (C1).
- League chase cost 37.1 to 43.9 runs per 600 PA decomposes as opportunities per PA +6%, chase rate +8%, cost per chase +3%. The cost-per-chase rise is concentrated in borderline chases (0.064 to 0.079 runs), the pitches the model most under-predicts as called strikes.
- The league's far-off chase rate (more than 0.35 feet off the recorded box) went 19.3% to 21.8%, and the far-off share of out-of-zone pitches 61.7% to 64.1%. Every card's season trend and every frozen 2027 cell is measured against a different box in 2026 than in 2021 to 2025, and no card says so.
- The called-strike model's direction error more than doubled. Actual minus predicted called strikes per 1,000 takes: borderline above +198 (2025: +4), borderline below +144 (−20), just-off above +117 (+64), just-off side −58 (−20).
- Per hitter, the direction-blind model overcharges chases by 0.83 ± 0.40 runs per 600 PA in 2026 against 0.28 ± 0.17 in 2025 (hitters with 300+ PA). The league mean cancels against the baseline; the spread does not. That is about one xChase+ point for a typical hitter and about three at the extremes: Cedric Mullins, Patrick Bailey and Moisés Ballesteros are overcharged by 1.9 to 2.2 runs, Spencer Steer undercharged by 0.4. The README's "roughly 0.2 to 0.4 runs per 600 PA" was right for 2025 and is about 2.4 times too low for 2026.
- The 2025-to-2026 reliability pair (0.70) is in range, so the ranking survived; but "comparable across seasons" leans on a 2026 league scale that is inflated partly by bookkeeping.

**E3. The fixes.** Add the side of the zone a pitch missed on (above, below, side) to the called-strike model, at least from 2026, and rerun. Rewrite the limitation with E1's numbers. Put the box change on the 2026 cards' trend and in the frozen 2027 file's notes: the cells are defined by the ABS box, which is fine if 2027 keeps it, and the file should say that.
