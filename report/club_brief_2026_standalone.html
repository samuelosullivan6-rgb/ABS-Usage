ABS challenge judgment

What the challenge system reveals about a hitter's eyes, when a token is worth spending, and how to tell an eyes problem from an approach problem. Part one is for the dugout. Part two is the evidence.

Part one · for the dugout

What to do first

Before any model: where the umpire's misses went unchallenged in 2026, counted straight from the feeds with the true location of every pitch. These numbers need no belief about anyone's eyes. "Runs left" prices every unchallenged miss at its stake, an upper bound since no one can see every miss, but the same yardstick for every team.

  1. Challenge more. Batters challenged 19% of the 11,992 true balls called strikes while they held a token, leaving about 40 runs per team-season; catchers challenged 32% of 10,444 true strikes called balls, leaving 32. Every one of those is a call the system would have flipped. Teams ranged from 12% to 26% of their hitters' misses challenged (median 20%).
  2. The high strike is the one to challenge. Umpires call strikes on 49% of takes in the first inch above the ABS box, against 40% off the side and 40% below it, so a high called strike is the likeliest to be an ABS ball. Hitters challenge only 15% of high misses against 26% of low ones. The box's top sits below the zone umpires call; tell hitters that in those words.
  3. Strike three on a true ball went unchallenged 0.14 times per team-game with a token in hand (688 in the season, 7.3 runs per team-season). Two-strike misses carry the biggest stakes on this page; a two-strike take that looked like a ball should always be asked about.
  4. Stop challenging pitches that were strikes by an inch or more. Of the 2,438 batter challenges that lost, the median was 1.2 inches inside the zone, and 36% were low pitches against 25% of all decisions: hitters over-challenge low and under-challenge high.
  5. Beyond two inches the miss is visible; inside an inch it mostly is not. Hitters challenged 15% of misses in the first inch, 23% in the second and 31% beyond that, leaving 25.2, 10.6 and 4.5 runs per team-season. The second inch is where a hitter who knows the box can gain the most.

Part one · for the dugout

When to spend a token

A challenge token is not worth a fixed amount. It is worth what it can still buy over the rest of the game. At first pitch the first token is worth about 0.15 runs and the second about 0.09; by the 9th the last token is worth about 0.04. Savant's breakeven rule prices every token at 0.20 runs. For a league-average eye that is more than a token is worth at any point in the game, so the rule is too stingy throughout: challenge more readily than it says, early as well as late, and more readily still as the game goes on.

  1. Early, with both tokens, a 33% chance the call flips is enough on a typical pitch. With the last token early, about 44%. Savant's price asks for 51%. To an average eye that is a pitch that looks about 1.7 inches off the plate with two tokens and 3.2 with one.
  2. Strike three and ball four are the big stakes. On a 3-2 count with runners on a flip is worth half a run, and early with both tokens a 16% chance is enough.
  3. In the 9th with your last token, about 19% is enough. An unused token at the final out is worth nothing, and a team at zero gets one back if the game goes on.
  4. Extra innings give a team at zero one token back each inning. A token in an extra inning is worth about 0.04 runs, the same as in the 9th, so the 9th-inning rule is the rule for the rest of the game.
  5. These numbers are for league-average eyes. Sharper eyes make a token worth more: with perceptual noise of 1.5 inches instead of 2.7, the first token at first pitch is worth 0.23 runs, above Savant's price, and early in the game the bar moves back toward Savant's.

Revised 2026-10-23. An earlier version of this page valued the first token at 0.49 runs and said to be stingy early. That came from a belief model in which a challenger who felt 90% sure was right about half the time. With beliefs that know how often the umpire is right, the first token is worth 0.15 and the early advice reverses.

Hitter challenge cards

Every called strike a hitter takes with a token in hand is a test of his eyes: the true location is known, and he either challenges or he doesn't. The eyes score is how sharply his challenge rate rises as the pitch moves from inside the zone to outside it, against the league. The criterion is how freely he spends tokens. The margins are how far off the plate a pitch should look to him before he challenges on a typical pitch, early with both tokens, early with the last token, mid-game, and in the 9th.

Read the eyes score as a range, not a rank: the band is the hitter's percentile among the league's hitters, plus or minus one posterior SD. Below 8 challenges it is not shown. Willingness to challenge (Spends pct) is a stable trait within a season (reliability 0.78); accuracy is not yet measurable per hitter, so the sort below makes no eyes call this season.

Eyes case or approach case

A hitter who chases has one of two problems. Either he does not see the pitch well enough to know it is a ball, or he sees it fine and swings anyway. The fix differs: pitch-recognition work for the first, a count plan for the second. Chase rate alone cannot separate them because every swing mixes perception with intent. The challenge decision on a take has no swing in it.

The sort uses chase rate and whichever judgment score the season can support. An eyes-or-approach call needs the eyes score's reliability to clear 0.5 at the low end of its interval, and this season it does not at any volume tested: even among the 29 hitters with 10+ challenges in each half it is 0.63 [0.21, 0.83]. So no hitter is called an eyes case or an approach case. A hitter in the top 40% of chase rate is sorted on the criterion instead, which is reliable (0.78): Chaser, free with tokens (spends tokens more freely than the median regular: aggressive on challenges as well as swings, which points to the approach) or Chaser, sparing with tokens (aggressive on swings only; whether the eyes are the reason stays open). Hitters in the bottom 40% of chase rate are Disciplined.

What the evidence supports. Sharper eyes on takes go with chasing less (correlation -0.18 across the 223 rated regulars, stronger among frequent challengers) and with cutting the chase rate from the prior season. They do not go with cheaper chases, better two-strike contact or fewer strikeouts once chase rate is held fixed. So the sort tells you which lever moves the rate; it does not say who pays most per chase.

Measurement

A hierarchical logit of challenge on true edge distance gives each hitter a slope (eyes) and an intercept (criterion), net of the side of the box the pitch missed on: at the same distance a low called strike is challenged far more often than a high one (+0.54 logits for a low pitch and −0.28 for a high one, against a pitch off the side; +0.66 for a corner). League slope 0.63 per inch; between-hitter spread 0.18 on the slope and 0.67 on the criterion, correlated +0.28 across hitters. Two strikes shift the criterion by 1.9 logits; a late inning by 0.7.

What the belief model does

The challenger's belief is the probability the call flips given the edge they perceive. The calibrated belief is fit on the opportunities themselves, so it knows the umpire is usually right: in every fifth of its range it is within 0.3 points of the true flip rate. The flat-prior belief, P = Φ(perceived edge / noise), was used on this page before 2026-10-23. It ignores the umpire's base rate, and a pitch it calls 80 to 100% likely flips about half the time.

Methods and caveats

Data
MLB StatsAPI live feeds carry every ABS challenge at the pitch level with the initiating player and outcome (review type MJ). Per-team counts reconcile with the in-game token ledger in all but one game. Statcast CSVs supply run values and bat tracking, joined on every pitch.
Zone
The feed's strike-zone bounds are the fixed ABS zone (53.5% and 27% of measured height). Ball location is solved at the plate midpoint from the nine-parameter trajectory; the signed edge distance uses a 2.9-inch ball and reproduces Savant's values exactly. It agrees with the ABS ruling on 99.99% of challenged pitches.
Decision set
Umpire-called strikes the hitter took with a token in hand (counting the extra-innings top-up), from 4 inches inside the edge to 6 outside: 65,940 pitches, 4,683 challenged. The window is wider on the ball side because every batter challenge lies within 6 inches outside, and those pitches are the only view of the top half of the curve.
Judgment model
Logit of challenge on edge distance with a per-hitter random intercept and slope under a full-covariance prior, fit by two-step empirical Bayes; count, token and leverage shifters and the side of the box the pitch missed on (above, below, side, corner) as fixed effects. Direction-by-distance slopes were tested and left out (chi-square 22 on 3 df, below the BIC price of 33). A hitter's slope and intercept posteriors correlate +0.20 at the median. The slope is unidentified for hitters who rarely challenge.
Token value
Backward induction over eight decision slots per half-inning for nine innings, with opportunities arriving at the observed rate (1.34 per team-half-inning: taken pitches within 3 inches of the edge, both sides) and stakes from Statcast run expectancy. Extra innings enter as a continuation value at the last out of the 9th: 209 of 2,429 games went to the 10th, each extra inning is followed by another with probability 0.27, and a team at zero gets one token back at the start of every extra inning, as the ledgers show. The simulated rules play the same token rule.
Belief
A challenger perceives the true edge plus Gaussian noise and holds the calibrated posterior P(flip | perceived edge), a logit of the true flip on the perceived edge fit with the same noise draw. The per-hitter margins use the same fit at each hitter's own noise level.
Caveats
One season of ABS. Run units, not win probability, so late close games are undervalued. The simulated rules use league-average Gaussian perception; the observed row is real players, who know more than that model. Under calibrated beliefs the token's value depends on the noise level, which is an upper bound. The eyes score is not reliable per hitter at any volume one season gives (0.63 [0.21, 0.83] at 10+ challenges per half), so it is shown as a range and drives no label. Year-over-year judgment and outcome share a season.
